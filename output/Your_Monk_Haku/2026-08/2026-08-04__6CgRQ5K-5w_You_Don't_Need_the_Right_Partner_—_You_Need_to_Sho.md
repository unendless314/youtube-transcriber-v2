# You Don't Need the Right Partner — You Need to Show Up as You Are

[00:00] Most people can tell you exactly when they fell in love. Almost no one can tell you whether it will last. A gardener knows why. He can bind two branches together in a single afternoon, whether they become one tree that takes years to find out, and no one, not even him, can rush it.
[00:15] Every person who has ever bound one branch to another has felt the same quiet fear in the moment before the cut. What if this is wrong? Not spoken aloud, not admitted, even to the one holding the other end of the branch. Only felt, privately, as the knife touches bark, a doubt that arrives at the exact moment two lives are about to be joined. I want to tell you something that took me many years and many failed graphs to understand. That doubt was never a sign that the joining was wrong. It was the first honest thing either branch had said to the other. Certainty is not what begins a true joining. Surrender is. And surrender, by its nature, cannot arrive dressed as certainty. It arrives dressed as not knowing, and most people mistake the costume for the truth underneath it. Before the cut, most people carry a shape in their mind of the branch they are looking for. They call it a checklist, though they rarely say the word aloud. A certain height, a certain
[01:13] steadiness, a way of speaking, a way of showing love, a set of wounds the other person must not have, and a set of virtues they must. I have listened to many people describe, in careful detail, the tree they hope to graft themselves onto, and I have noticed that the detail itself is the first problem. A checklist is not wisdom, it is fear wearing the clothing of preparation. It says, if I specify precisely enough what I need, I will be protected from the pain of choosing wrong.
[01:40] But wood does not care what you specified. Two branches either run sap together or they do not, and no list drawn up in advance, however careful, can determine that in the absence of the joining itself. I have watched people reject a perfectly compatible branch because it failed to match an item on a list written years earlier by a person who did not yet know what union actually required of them, and I have watched that same list quietly dissolve, month by month. Once real joining began, not because the person gave up their standards, but because they discovered the list was never describing the branch. It was describing their own fear of being hurt again. This is not an argument against knowing yourself or against having true limits. It is a warning about the difference between a limit, which protects what is sacred in you, and a checklist, which merely tries to guarantee an outcome that cannot be guaranteed. The illusion is not that you deserve care. The illusion is that you
[02:35] can outplan uncertainty. You cannot. The gardener with the longest list of requirements still does not know, on the day of the binding, whether the graft will take. He only knows a little more about what he was afraid of. Here is what surprises most people once the binding has actually taken place.
[02:53] The friction does not stop. If anything, it begins in earnest only now, once the branch is truly attached and the wind is free to test it. Two woods pressed together for the first time do not simply agree with each other. The grain runs in different directions. The growth rates differ. What one branch calls thriving, the trunk may briefly experience as strain. I want you to hear this clearly, because it is the point at which most grafts are abandoned unnecessarily. Friction is not the tree telling you it made a mistake. Friction is the tree telling you it is alive, and that two different histories are now occupying the same trunk, and learning, slowly, how to share it. If there were no friction, I would be far more suspicious. It would mean one side had simply stopped growing, or had shaped itself entirely around the other, rather than joining it as a separate living thing. I met a couple once, who came to me certain their marriage was failing, because they had begun, in the second year, to disagree about matters
[03:52] they had never disagreed about before. I did not tell them this was good news, because grief rarely wants to hear good news in the moment it is grieving. But I asked them to notice something. The disagreements had not appeared, because they had grown apart. They had appeared, because each had finally stopped performing agreement long enough to reveal an actual separate self underneath. The friction was not the crack in the graft. The friction was the first evidence that two real trees, and not two masks, were now attempting to grow as one. So when the wind finds the place where you are joined to another person, and something in you tenses and says, "This must mean it is wrong," pause before you believe it. Ask instead whether this is the sound of two real, separate lives learning to hold weight together. That sound is rarely comfortable. It is very often exactly what growth requires. Underneath both the checklist and the friction lies something I think is worth naming directly, because I do not believe it is spoken about honestly enough. What
[04:50] most people are actually seeking, when they seek the right person, is not a person at all. It is safety from uncertainty itself. They believe that if they choose correctly, they will be relieved, permanently, of the fear that accompanies loving someone whose future they cannot control. I have never seen a graft that offered this. Even the deepest joining I have witnessed decades of shared root, shared shade, shared storm survived, did not eliminate uncertainty. It simply changed what the two branches did with it.
[05:18] They did not stop being separate, mortal, changeable things. They learned instead to remain present with each other, despite that. This, I think, is closer to the truth than any list of qualities could bring you.
[05:29] The task was never to find a branch, guaranteed not to disappoint you, injure you, or someday be lost to you. No such branch exists, on any tree, in any orchard. The task was to become the kind of trunk capable of staying rooted and present, while joined to something whose future, like your own, cannot be fully known in advance. This is not resignation. It is not settling. It is, if anything, a far more demanding practice than searching for the perfect branch, because it asks something of you, rather than something of the other person. It asks whether you can remain steady, awake, and generous toward what is actually growing beside you, rather than mourning the absence of a branch you only ever held in your imagination. I will not tell you, today, how to know for certain that you have found the right person.
[06:17] I do not believe that certainty was ever available to anyone standing at the moment of the cut, and I think the demand for it has caused more suffering than the uncertainty itself ever could.
[06:26] What I will ask you, before you leave this place, is something quieter? What is the list you are still holding, not only in matters of love, though certainly there, but in your friendships, your work, your own quiet expectations of how life was supposed to arrange itself before you could finally feel at peace? What is the precise shape you have been waiting for reality to take, and what would it cost you to set that shape down, even for a season, and look instead at what has actually grown beside you?
[06:54] The gardener does not ask the graft to prove itself before he commits to caring for it. He commits, and then he tends what actually grows, season after season, whether or not it resembles what he first imagined. The tree does not ask to be certain. It only continues, faithfully, to see what grows.
