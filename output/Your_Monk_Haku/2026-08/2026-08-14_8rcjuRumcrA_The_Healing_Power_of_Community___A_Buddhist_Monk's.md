# The Healing Power of Community | A Buddhist Monk's Story of Two Neighbors

[00:24] We tell ourselves a story about peace. We believe that peace is something we find alone. A quiet room, a closed door, a life arranged so carefully that no one else can disturb its surface. I understand the appeal of this story because I lived inside it for many years, before I understood what it was costing me. When I was a younger monk, I believed that solitude was the highest practice. I thought that the fewer people I needed, the closer I was to freedom. I would walk past the other monks in the corridor, and feel a quiet pride that I required so little from them. I ate alone when I could. I meditated in the far room, away from the others, because I told myself their presence was a distraction from something purer. What I did not see then was that I had not built peace. [01:21] It was nothing dramatic, a fever, a cough that would not leave. Three days where I could not stand long enough to prepare my own food. I lay on my mat and understood, with a clarity that frightened me, that I had spent years perfecting my own absence from other people's lives. [01:55] He did not scold me. He simply sat beside my mat, put a hand on my forehead, and said nothing for a long while. Then he told me something I have never forgotten. Instead, you filled it with more solitude." I tell you this because I think many of us are building the same wall, in smaller and quieter ways. We call it independence. We call it not wanting to be a burden. We call it being fine on our own. And sometimes, yes, this is simply true.
[02:33] Some solitude is necessary. Some quiet is medicine. But often, if we are honest, what we call independence is really a fear, dressed in respectable clothing. We do not heal in isolation nearly as often or as completely as we imagine we will. Something happens in the presence of others, something we cannot manufacture alone. No matter how disciplined our solitude, no matter how well we have arranged our quiet rooms, there is a kind of mending that only occurs between two people, in the space between them, and not within either one alone. In the teaching of my tradition, there is an idea called patika samupada, which is usually translated as dependent origination, though I find that translation a little cold for what it actually describes. What it means, at its heart, is this: nothing arises alone, nothing exists as an island unto itself,
[07:56] sealed off, self-created, self-sustained. Every flame depends on air, every wave depends on the ocean it rises from and returns to. And every human being, whether we admit it or not, depends on other human beings. Not as a weakness to be overcome, but as the very fabric out of which a life is woven. We resist this teaching, especially in places and times that prize independence as the highest virtue. We want to believe we are self-made, self-sufficient, complete on our own. And in small doses, this belief can motivate real strength. But taken too far, it becomes its own kind of suffering. Because it asks us to carry alone what was never meant to be carried alone. Consider what happened to the woman under the beam. Her body was in danger. Yes, that is obvious. But something else was happening too. Something less visible. For eleven years, she had been carrying a private weight. The weight of an unresolved wound with her neighbour. [08:54] She had told herself this was simply how her life was now. She had made peace, she thought, with living alone in more ways than one. But suffering carried in isolation does not actually shrink. It only becomes quieter, more hidden, more disguised as normal. This is one of the harder truths I have come to understand, that isolation does not resolve pain, it simply removes the witnesses to it. Even while we walk around telling everyone, including ourselves, that we are fine. What her neighbour's hands did that night, reaching through the dark and the dust, was more than a physical rescue. It was a form of witnessing. It said, without a single word, I see you, even now, even after everything, and I am choosing you anyway. [09:52] not fall because of an apology. It fell because another person proved, with their own bleeding hands, that the wall had never actually been necessary. This is the counter-intuitive part, and I want to say it plainly, because I think we often get this backwards. We assume that healing must happen first, privately, within ourselves, before we are ready to let others close again. We think, I will fix myself, and then I will return to my relationships whole. But so often, in my experience of sitting with people in their grief, in their shame, in their long private struggles, the order runs the other way. It is often the return to another person, imperfect, unfinished, still wounded, that becomes the very thing that makes the healing possible in the first place. We do not heal, and then reconnect. A fact that, once you hear it, may change the way you think about every wound you have ever tried to heal, completely alone. Researchers who study wound healing have observed, across a number of controlled studies, that the presence or absence of felt social connection can measurably influence how quickly a physical wound closes. Something as unglamorous and biological as a cut on the skin appears to care whether or not you feel accompanied. I find this almost startling, because we tend to draw a firm line between the physical and the emotional, as though the body operates on one set of rules and the heart on another, entirely separate set. But the body, it turns out, is listening to our sense of belonging far more closely than we assumed. Isolation appears to place the body under a kind of low, persistent stress, and that stress interferes with the very processes that are supposed to be knitting the wound back together.
