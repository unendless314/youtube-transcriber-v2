# Grieving Someone Who Is Still Alive (When They Stop Calling)

[00:15] That is what makes it so hard to explain. There was no funeral, no casseroles left at the door, no one saying, I'm so sorry for your loss. There was only a slow thinning. [01:50] They call it ghosting. It is an odd word when you think about it. A ghost is someone who has died and cannot leave. But the one who went quiet is not the ghost. You are the one who is haunted.
[02:02] You are the one who wanders the same rooms, checking the same places, listening for something that does not come. I want to be careful here. [02:26] But there is something about this experience that has puzzled me for many years, watching people come to the temple carrying this particular ache. When someone dies, the grief is enormous, but it is clean. It has edges. It has a beginning, and even if the ending is far away, you can feel that there is one. This grief has no edges. It seeps. It shows up in the grocery store, in a song, in the way a certain kind of afternoon light falls across a table. And many of you have told me, very quietly, that this is worse than a death. You say it as if confessing something shameful, as if you had no right to feel it. I want you to hear me clearly. It is not a sign that you are weak, or that you are being dramatic. It is a clue. Something about this kind of loss is different in its very structure. And until we understand that structure, we will keep fighting a shadow. So let us look at it together. Slowly. [03:26] strangest part of all, the part that almost no one says aloud, why the unanswered can hurt more than even the hardest answer. There was a woman who used to come to the temple on Tuesday mornings. Her name was Fumiko. She was 64 years old, and for 31 years she had been friends with a woman she had known since they were girls in the same lane, sharing the same well, and later, the same griefs. Then one spring, the friend stopped answering. There had been no quarrel. There had been no cold word. Fumiko wrote a gentle message, and then another, and then another. Nothing came back. Not anger. Not even a refusal. Only the quiet. Fumiko began keeping her phone on the kitchen table, face up, fully charged, even at night. She told me she had stopped going to the market at her usual hour, in case the call came while she was out. She was 64 years old, and she was arranging her whole life around a small machine that did not ring. One Tuesday I asked

Then she said very quietly, "I don't think I want to talk to her. I think I want to know why."
[04:53] And I said, "The phone can only carry her voice. It cannot carry the reason. She may not even know it herself." Fumiko cried then, not loudly, but the way a person cries when something they have been holding up for a very long time is finally allowed to rest on the ground. Here is what I understand about the unanswered: A no is painful, but a no is a flaw. It tells you where you stand. You can rage against it. You can weep on it. And in time you turn and walk toward another door. A closed door is honest. It lets you leave. But an unanswered message is a door left swinging in the wind. You cannot walk away because it might open. You cannot rest because it might slam. Every small sound makes you look up. The mind is a creature built to finish sentences, and this kind of silence hands it a sentence with no end. So the mind keeps reaching for the ending, night after night, and never once finds it. [05:54] we call a gentle thing, can be cruel here. Grief that is allowed to finish is like a river reaching the sea. But grief held open by hope is a river that has lost its way to the sea.

[06:29] It was a small act, and it was the first honest one she had taken in a year. But it did not quiet her, because a phone in a drawer is silent, and the mind is not. Silence is a blank page. That is the first thing to understand. A silence has no handwriting of its own. It cannot say a word about you. And the mind, which cannot bear a blank page, picks up a pen.
[07:04] When I was twenty-six, I trained beside a monk named Ryokan. We shared a small room and a great many cold mornings. He could make me laugh so hard that tea came out of my nose. And I do not think there has been a day since when I did not remember that. One winter, he left. There was no farewell. I woke one morning, and his sandals were gone from beside the door. I wrote to him for two years, and no letter ever came back. During those years, I wrote a story about why. In my story, I had said something careless at tea the evening before he left. I could almost hear the words, though I could never quite recall what they were. That is the strange craft of the mind. It will invent evidence. It will produce a memory that never happened, and hand it to you as if it were a fact. And that story changed me. I became more careful, more guarded. I stopped letting anyone close at the temple. Nobody had said a word to me, and yet I had passed a sentence on myself, and served it for years. Twenty years later, I met a monk who
[08:12] had known Ryokan. He told me that Ryokan's father had been dying when he left, and that he had felt so ashamed of abandoning his family for the robe that he could not face anyone who had known him in that life.

[08:45] It reflects only the hand that holds the pen. The Buddha spoke of two arrows. The first arrow is the pain of what happens. They stopped calling. That arrow has landed, and you cannot unshoot it.

[10:49] When someone goes quiet, we grieve a version of them. We grieve the one who remembered our birthday, who knew the name of our dog, who finished our sentences at the table.
