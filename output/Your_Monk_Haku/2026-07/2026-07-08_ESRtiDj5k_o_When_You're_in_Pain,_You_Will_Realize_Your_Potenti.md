# When You're in Pain, You Will Realize Your Potential | Buddhist Monk Wisdom

[00:00] There is a stand of bamboo near the temple where I was trained, and every winter it bends nearly to the ground under snow that no one asked it to carry. For years I watched this happen and thought it was suffering. The bamboo looked bowed, almost broken, folded low as though something had defeated it overnight. I did not yet understand what I was looking at. What I did not see, because I was only looking at the bending, was the root. Beneath the surface, where no wind reaches and no eye can follow, the root was widening, spreading further than it needed to when the sky was clear and the days were easy. The bending was not the injury, the bending was the method. We are taught, so often, to fear the moment we are bent. A diagnosis, a door closing, a person who leaves without explanation. We call this pain, and we are not wrong to call it that, but we are often wrong about what it means. We assume it means something has gone wrong with us. We rarely
[00:57] consider that it might mean something is finally being asked of us. I think of the people who come to me after loss, sitting with their hands folded the way mine are folded now, waiting for me to tell them the pain will end soon. I do not tell them that. I tell them something quieter, and often less welcome. The pain is not a mistake in your life. It is not evidence that you did something wrong, or that the universe has singled you out for cruelty. It is weight. And weight, like snow on bamboo, has a strange relationship with strength. It does not simply crush. It also asks the roots a question they were never asked while the sky was clear. Most of us go years without being asked that question.
[01:38] We move through comfortable seasons, unaware of how shallow our roots have grown, because nothing has required them to grow deeper. We call this peace, and in a sense it is. But it is also a kind of sleep.
[01:51] The bamboo in summer does not know its own strength, because summer has never demanded it. This is not a teaching that pain is good. I would not insult you by saying that. A death is still a death. A broken trust is still broken. I am only asking you to notice something that grief tends to hide from us in the moment it is happening. That the very thing bending you low is in the same instant asking something enormous of what lies beneath you, not instead of the pain, inside it. You do not have to believe this yet. Belief is not required for it to be true. I only ask that you remain curious about your own roots. Even while your back is bent low beneath something heavy. Something is happening down there that the snow cannot see, and neither for now can you. There is a particular kind of person who has never been tested, and you can often recognize them by how certain they are about themselves. It is simply the nature of untested ground.
[02:49] A person who has only known ease has a theory of who they are, built entirely from imagination, because comfort has never once asked them to prove it. They believe they are patient, because nothing has yet made them wait in agony. They believe they are strong, because nothing has yet tried to break them. This is not dishonesty.
[03:08] It is simply a self that has not yet been asked a real question. I was such a person once, before my training, before I understood that comfort is not the absence of struggle, so much as the postponement of it. In comfort, potential sleeps. It does not vanish. It only waits, the way a seed waits in dry soil, fully capable of a life it has not yet been asked to begin. This is difficult to hear, because we have been told, endlessly, that the good life is the comfortable one. And there is truth thereto: I do not romanticize suffering, and any teacher who does should not be trusted. But comfort, left alone for too long, has a quiet cost. It never reveals us to ourselves. We can live an entire life beside our own potential, without ever meeting it. The way two strangers can share a train car for years, and never once speak.
[04:01] Pain, in this sense, functions almost like an introduction we did not ask for. It forces a meeting.
[04:08] The version of you that exists only in comfortable theory, is suddenly required to show up in practice, under conditions no one would choose. This is often the first time a person discovers a capacity for patients they did not know they had, or a clarity that arrives only once the noise of an easy life has been stripped away. I think of a woman who once sat across from me, several years ago now, describing the year she lost both her mother, and her position at the company she had built her identity around.
[04:35] She told me, almost apologetically, that some part of her, a small quiet part she was ashamed of, had discovered something in that year, not gratitude for the loss. She was careful to say that, but a discovery. She had found a steadiness in herself that had simply never been called upon before, because nothing had ever asked her to hold that much at once.
[04:58] This is what comfort conceals, not through cruelty, but through mercy. It does not test us because it does not need to. But somewhere beneath the surface of an easy life, capacities remain folded, unused, waiting for a season that has not yet arrived. You may already be carrying more than you believe, you have simply never been asked to unfold it.
[05:20] There is an old distinction, one I return to often, between what happens to us and what happens through us. Pain is something that happens to us. This is undeniable, and I would not ask you to pretend otherwise. But there is a second, quieter process available to us, one that most people discover by accident rather than intention. It is the process of pain becoming pressure, and pressure, given time and attention, becoming transformation. Not because pain is good, but because pressure, applied to something with enough integrity to withstand it, tends to clarify what was already there. Coal becomes nothing new under pressure. It does not become something foreign to itself. It becomes more purely what it already was, compressed until the impurities fall away, and what remains is harder, clearer, capable of holding light in a way. Loose coal never could. The pressure did not add anything. It only removed what was never essential. I do not say this to make pain sound noble. Suffering is not a machine we can control,
[06:24] engineering hardship in the hope of becoming diamonds. That would be its own kind of arrogance, and life rarely cooperates with such plans. What I am describing is something humbler and more honest, that when pain arrives uninvited, unchosen, often unwanted, it carries within it an opportunity that comfort does not offer. The opportunity to discover, under pressure, what in you was never negotiable.
[06:49] This is the quiet centre of everything I am trying to say to you. Not that pain builds character, which is a phrase too worn to mean much anymore. But that pain reveals what was already load-bearing in you, the parts of your character that were never decorative, never dependent on easy conditions to remain standing. You do not build these things in the moment of pressure. You only discover finally that they were there all along. I think of the bamboo again. The pressure of the snow does not create strength in the root system. The root system was already capable of this. The snow only forces the plant to use what it already possessed, and had never yet been required to spend. So when pain arrives in your life, and it will, again and again, in forms you cannot yet predict, I would ask you to hold this thought lightly, without forcing it, somewhere beneath what is breaking, something is also being asked to reveal itself. Not created, revealed, this is the whole of the teaching, spoken as plainly as I know how to say it.
[07:52] After the pressure, after the bending, something arrives that could not have arrived any other way, this is the part people are often reluctant to speak about, because it can sound, if said carelessly, like gratitude for suffering. It is not that. It is something closer to inventory, a quiet accounting of what remains once the worst of it has passed. I have sat with many people, in the weeks after their worst season, and there is a particular kind of stillness in them, that I do not see in people, who have only known comfort. It is not happiness. Grief does not simply convert into joy, no matter what certain teachers promise, but there is a clarity that was not present before, a capacity for compassion that was, until then, theoretical, a tolerance for other people's suffering, that could not have been manufactured, only earned through having once knelt in the same place. This is one of the strange mercies hidden inside pain. Not that it feels merciful while it is happening, but that it leaves behind
[08:55] capacities we did not possess before. A person who has survived real fear develops a different relationship to other people's fear. A person who has been abandoned develops an instinct for recognizing loneliness in a room, long before anyone speaks of it. These are not small things. They are forms of potential that comfort, for all its gifts, rarely produces on its own. I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to make this sound like a trade worth seeking. It is not. I do not wish pain upon anyone, and I would not ask you to welcome it as a teacher you invited into your home. But when it arrives, as it does for every person without exception, I want you to know that something is being asked of you beneath the surface. Something that may not become visible until much later, in a conversation you have not yet had, with a person you have not yet met, who is suffering in a way only you, because of what you carried, will know how to sit beside. This is what the break reveals. Not a lesson delivered neatly,
[09:55] like a moral at the end of a story. Something quieter, a widened capacity, discovered only because the old, narrower one was no longer sufficient for what you were being asked to hold. I do not know what will bend you next. Neither do you, most likely, and this not knowing is itself part of what we are asked to carry. The bamboo does not know, each winter, how much snow is coming, or whether this will be the season that finally proves too heavy. It bends anyway. It has no other honest option. What I can offer you is not a promise that the bending will stop, because I would be lying if I said such a thing.
[10:32] What I can offer is smaller, and perhaps more useful. The roots you have already grown, in seasons you may not even remember clearly now, are still there, widened by winters you have already survived, whether or not you ever noticed the widening while it was happening. I think sometimes of how little the bamboo seems to know about its own strength while the snow is falling. It only knows the weight, the bending, the uncertainty of whether this is the season it finally breaks. The strength is invisible to it, in the moment it is most needed. Perhaps this is true of us as well. Perhaps we are rarely permitted to see our own potential while we are in the middle of discovering it. We only see it afterward, if at all, and even then only partially, the way you might notice a scar and forget, for a moment the wound that made it. I will not tell you that the next hard season will feel different because of what I have said today. It may feel exactly the same. Heavy, unwelcome, entirely too large for whatever strength
