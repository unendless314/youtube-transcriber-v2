# A Buddhist Monk's Truth About Self-Improvement

[00:00] There is a bowl in my room that a friend gave me many years ago. It fell once from a low shelf, and a crack ran across its base like a river, finding its path across dry land. I did not throw it away. I did not hide it. I have used it every day since, and the tea inside it has never once spilled through that crack, because the crack does not reach all the way through.
[00:22] I begin with this bowl because most people who come to me speak of self-improvement the way they would speak of that crack, as a floor to be erased, a fault line to be sealed shut, a mistake in the clay that should never have happened. They want to sand it down. They want to fire it again in a hotter kiln. They believe that somewhere underneath the crack is the bowl they were supposed to be, and that all their effort should go toward uncovering it.
[00:47] But I have held this bowl in the morning light, and I have held it in the dark, and I can tell you there is no other bowl underneath. There is only this one, crack and all, and it holds tea exactly as well as it did the day it was made.
[01:00] This is not a teaching about accepting flaws and calling it wisdom. That is too easy, and it is not what I mean. I mean something stranger. I mean that the very idea of a better bowl hiding beneath the flawed bowl is the first illusion we must look at closely, because it assumes a self that exists, finished and correct, waiting to be revealed once the improving is done.
[01:21] And when you search for that finished self, when you really search, the way you might run your fingers along the inside of the bowl looking for a seam, you find nothing. You find only the searching.
[01:33] In the days ahead, I want to walk with you through what we are actually doing when we say we want to improve ourselves. We will meet a gardener who pulls up his seedlings each morning to check if the roots have grown.
[01:44] We will sit beside a river and ask it what it thinks of its own current. We will stand before a mirror that has grown tired of looking at itself.
[01:52] And we will return at the end to this bowl, not because I will finally tell you what to do with your own cracks, but because by then you may no longer need me to.
[02:00] For now, only this. The bowl does not know it is cracked, it only knows it is being used. And perhaps the tea does not care what shape carried it to your lips. Only that it was carried. Only that it was warm. Only that for a moment someone drank.
[02:18] I knew a man once, I will not say where, only that he lived by a small plot of land, who wanted, more than anything, to grow a single perfect radish.
[02:27] Not many radishes. One. He believed that if he could grow one radish without blemish, without crack, without the small imperfections that mark every living thing pulled from soil, he would finally understand what it meant to grow something well.
[02:42] Each morning he went to the plot before the sun had fully risen, and he pulled the seedling gently from the earth to examine its roots. Were they straight enough? Were they pale enough? Had they grown in the night the way he had hoped?
[02:54] And each evening, seeing that the roots looked much the same as they had that morning, he placed the seedling back into the soil, patted it down, and told himself that tomorrow would show more progress.
[03:05] "You already see the problem, and I will not insult you by explaining it further than this. A seedling examined every day never becomes a radish; it becomes a seedling that is disturbed every day. The very act of checking undoes the growing. This is the shape of so much self-improvement.
[03:24] We plant an intention. To be more patient, more disciplined, more at peace, and then, before the roots have had a single night of undisturbed dark, we pull ourselves up by the collar to check our progress. Am I calmer than I was yesterday? Have I grown?
[03:40] We hold ourselves under the light of our own anxious inspection, and we mistake the inspection for the gardening.
[03:46] Here is the part one want you to sit with. Quietly, the way you might sit with a stone in your palm until it grows warm.
[03:54] The gardener did not fail, because he lacked discipline. He had tremendous discipline, more than most. He failed because he could not trust the soil to work in the dark.
[04:04] He needed to see the growing happen, and a thing that must be seen in order to be believed is rarely the thing that is actually growing.
[04:11] There is a kind of change that only happens when no one, not even you, is watching for it.
[04:16] You do not notice your own patience deepening any more than you notice your own hair growing.
[04:21] You simply turn one day and find that a grey thread has appeared, and you cannot say which morning it arrived.
[04:27] The radish, left alone, grew fine in the end, smaller than he had hoped, marked with one thin scar, where a stone had pressed against it as it swelled.
[04:38] He ate it without ceremony. He told me afterward that it was the best thing he had ever grown, and when I asked him why, he said only that it was the one year he had forgotten to check.
[04:48] Below the temple where I first studied, there was a river that had been cutting through stone for longer than anyone could say.
[04:55] In summer it ran fast and cold from the mountains.
[04:58] In autumn it slowed and grew the colour of tea.
[05:01] I used to sit beside it as a young monk, certain that if I watched long enough, I would understand something about patience, about persistence, about the quiet virtue of water wearing down what is hard.
[05:13] I no longer believe the river is patient. I no longer believe it is persistent. I do not think the river is trying to do anything at all.
[05:22] The river does not wake in the morning, and resolve to carve the stone a little deeper.
[05:27] It does not measure the canyon against yesterday's canyon, and feel dissatisfied with the pace of its own erosion.
[05:33] It simply moves, in the only way water can move when it meets stone, and the canyon is not the river's achievement.
[05:40] It is only what happened while the river was doing nothing but being water.
[05:44] I tell you this because so many of us have taken the river as a symbol of effort, when it may be a symbol of something closer to its opposite.
[05:51] We say be like water, and we mean it as encouragement, to try harder, softer, longer.
[05:57] But water does not try. Trying requires a self that stands apart from the doing and grades it.
[06:03] The river has no such self. There is no small voice in the current whispering.
[06:08] Is this canyon deep enough yet? Am I becoming the river I am meant to be?
[06:13] And here is the strange mercy in this. If you removed every trace of self-judgment from your own days, not the awareness, only the judgment, you would not become still.
[06:24] You would not stop moving toward whatever you are moving toward.
[06:27] You would simply move the way the river moves, meeting each stone as it comes, shaped by the meeting rather than anxious about it.
[06:35] I am not telling you to stop wanting to change. Wanting is not the problem.
[06:39] The problem is the second river we build alongside the first. An imagined river, always slightly ahead of us, cutting a cleaner canyon, moving with more grace, and against which we measure our own current and find it lacking.
[06:52] That second river does not exist. It never carved anything. It only stood on the bank, watching and calling itself improvement.
[07:00] The canyon here is very deep now. I have walked its length many times, and I have never once seen the river look back at what it made.
[07:08] There is an old story, told many ways in many places, of a young monk who polished a piece of tile every day, believing that with enough effort and enough years, the tile would become a mirror.
[07:21] His teacher found him at this task and asked what he was doing. When the young monk explained, the teacher began polishing the tile alongside him, and asked in return how polishing a tile could ever produce a mirror.
[07:33] The young monk understood, and gave up his work. I do not think he understood correctly. He heard the story as an instruction to stop striving. I hear it differently now. After enough years of my own tile and my own cloth. I think the teacher was not condemning the polishing. I think the teacher was pointing at the wrong object.
[07:52] You cannot polish a tile into a mirror, but you also cannot polish a mirror into a better mirror. A mirror that spends its days examining its own surface.
[08:03] Wondering if it reflects clearly enough. Wondering if it is a good mirror. A worthy mirror. An improved mirror. That mirror has stopped doing the one thing a mirror is for. It has turned inward, toward itself, when its entire purpose faces outward.
[08:16] This is close to the centre of what I want to say to you today, so I will say it plainly, and only once, and then let it rest. Most of what we call self-improvement is a mirror trying to see its own face. It cannot be done. Not because you are not capable, not because you lack the discipline of the gardener or the patience of the river, but because the instrument you are using to look is the same instrument you are trying to examine. The eye cannot see itself except in reflection, and the reflection is not the eye.
[08:45] And yet, this is not an argument for abandoning the mirror. The way the young monk abandoned his tile. A mirror is still worth having. It is only worth having for what it shows you of the room, not for what it thinks of itself.
[08:58] I have watched people spend decades polishing their own reflection, refining the story of who they are, adjusting the angle of the glass to catch better light, and all the while the room behind them, the actual life, the actual people needing actual attention, passed by unreflected because the mirror was turned the wrong way.
[09:17] I still have the bowl. It sits now where it has always sat. And this morning, before I came to speak with you, I filled it with tea the way I do every morning, and the crack held the way it has always held, and I drank from it without thinking about it at all. Which is, I think, the first time in this telling that the bowl has been used correctly.
[09:43] When used correctly, we have walked a strange path together. A gardener who could not stop checking his own roots. A river that improves nothing and carves everything. A mirror that mistook its own reflection for its purpose. And now back to the bowl, which I promised you I would not resolve, and I intend to keep that promise.
[10:01] I will not tell you to love your cracks. That instruction has been given so many times by so many teachers, that it has become its own kind of polishing, a way of examining the self, one more time, dressed up as acceptance instead of improvement, but still facing the same wrong direction, still asking the same question, what do I think of myself?
[10:22] Here is only what I have noticed after many years of tea. The bowl was never waiting to become something else. It was already doing the only thing it was ever going to do, from the moment it was fired, from the moment it cracked, from the moment it was placed back on the shelf instead of thrown away. It was holding what was poured into it. That is all. That is everything.
[10:43] Perhaps you came to this teaching wanting five steps. Some method for becoming a more improved version of the person sitting where you are sitting now.
[10:52] I have not given you that, and I am not going to, because I do not believe that person is waiting anywhere to be found. I believe there is only the pouring, and the holding, and the drinking, done again and again, in a shape that was never going to be perfect, and was never for one single morning unfit to be used.
[11:10] The tea in my bowl this morning was a little too hot. I drank it anyway. I did not think, even once, about the crack. That is where I will leave you, not at an answer, but at the shelf, in the early light, with your own bowl in your hands, and the question of what you will pour into it still entirely, quietly, yours.
