# You're not holding on too tight — you're holding on to the wrong thing

[00:00] There is something you are holding right now. You may not see it. You may not even feel it as a burden anymore, because you have carried it for so long that it has begun to feel like part of you. A memory that will not soften. A version of someone who hurt you, replayed so many times it has worn a groove into your mind. A version of yourself you cannot forgive.
[00:21] You hold it tightly, the way a hand closes around something fragile, afraid that if you loosen your grip, something worse will happen. But here is the quiet paradox that Buddhism has always known.
[00:34] The tighter you hold, the more certain you are that something will slip. The gripping itself becomes the source of the suffering. I want you to notice something. Think of a moment you are still carrying, a wound, a regret, a loss. Now imagine your hand closed around it, knuckles white, fingers pressed in. That image is not a metaphor. That is what the mind actually does. It contracts around pain as if contraction were protection. What we will explore together is not how to let go. That question, on its own, leads nowhere. Trying to release something by force is just another form of gripping.
[01:15] What we will explore instead is something quieter and more honest. Why holding on feels like safety.
[01:22] Because when you understand that, the release does not need to be forced. It happens the way a hand opens when it finally understands there is nothing left to protect. You are not broken for holding on, you are human. But there is a life waiting on the other side of this. And it begins not with effort, but with understanding. The Buddha taught that suffering does not come from pain itself.
[01:47] Pain is inevitable. It visits every life without exception. What the Buddha pointed to was something subtler. The second arrow. The first arrow is the wound, the loss, the betrayal, the failure.
[02:02] The second arrow is what we do afterward. We pick it up ourselves and press it deeper. We rehearse the pain.
[02:08] We narrate it. We build an identity around it. And we do this not because we are weak, but because the mind believes it is keeping us safe. When something hurts us, the mind treats it as data. It says, "Remember this. Analyze this. Do not let your guard down again." And so the memory is kept alive, turned over, examined from every angle, not out of cruelty to ourselves, but out of a misguided form of protection. The mind thinks that if it holds the wound close enough, it can prevent the next one.
[02:40] This is what Buddhism calls upadana, clinging. And it does not only apply to things we love.
[02:47] We cling to pain just as fiercely as we cling to pleasure. Because both feel like certainty in a world that offers none. The person who hurt you becomes something fixed and known in a universe that is constantly changing. The grief becomes familiar. The anger becomes a compass. And the mind, which fears the open water of uncertainty more than almost anything, chooses the known pain over the unknown peace.
[03:14] There is no judgment in this. Recognizing why we cling is not the same as excusing the harm we carry.
[03:21] It is simply the beginning of honesty. And honesty, in Buddhist practice, is always where freedom starts.
[03:28] Imagine standing at the edge of a river, reaching down and trying to hold the water still in your hands.
[03:35] You press your palms together. You squeeze. And still, between every finger, the river moves.
[03:41] Not because the river is cruel. Not because you are not trying hard enough. But because movement is the nature of water. Stillness was never what it was made for. The Buddhist teaching of Anika, impermanence, is often presented as a kind of warning. Everything changes. Nothing lasts. But I have found, after many decades of practice, that this teaching is not a warning at all. It is medicine.
[04:08] Because if everything is impermanent, then so is this. The pain you are holding is not a permanent feature of your inner landscape. It feels permanent because you have watered it with your attention every day. But the feeling itself, beneath the stories you have built around it, is as fluid as that river. It arose. Which means, by the very nature of things, it is capable of passing.
[04:31] The hand clenched around the water does not hold the river still. It only exhausts itself. And while the hand is clenched, it cannot receive anything new. This is one of the things people misunderstand about letting go. They imagine it means forgetting. Or pretending the pain did not matter. But the river does not forget the stones it has passed over. It carries their shape in its current. Letting go does not erase what happened. It simply stops freezing it in place. It allows what was real to move. The way all real things must move. Forward and through. And eventually, into something quieter than you expected.
[05:12] The stone does not disappear. But the water flows.
[05:18] I will not give you a list of instructions. The mind that is in pain does not need more tasks.
[05:24] What it needs is a different way of seeing. And so I want to offer you three shifts in perception, not steps, but openings. The first is this: separate the event from the story. What happened to you is real. But the version you carry in your mind has been edited by memory, shaped by emotion, and retold so many times it may no longer resemble what actually occurred. Begin to notice the difference between the original wound and the story your mind has constructed around it. The wound is a scar. The story is a hand pressing on it every day. You can choose, very slowly, to lift the hand. The second opening is compassion. Not for the person who hurt you, not yet, and perhaps not ever in the way people imagine, but for yourself. The part of you that is still holding on is not your enemy. It is a frightened part of you that believed this was the only way to stay safe. Meet it with the same gentleness you would offer a child who has been gripping something sharp for so long they no longer notice
[06:27] the bleeding. Compassion does not mean approval. It means you stop treating your own suffering as a character flaw. The third is perhaps the quietest. Sit with the open hand. In meditation, or simply in a still moment, let your hand rest open in your lap. Feel the difference between that openness and the sensation of gripping. You are not grasping for peace. You are simply making room for it. An open hand cannot force peace to arrive. But a closed hand guarantees it cannot enter. These are not remedies. They are orientations. And repeated gently, over time, they begin to change what the mind reaches for when it is afraid.
[07:11] You came to this teaching carrying something. I do not know its shape. I do not know how long you have held it, or how many times you have tried to put it down, only to find it back in your hands by morning.
[07:24] But I know this. The fact that you are still asking the question means the hand is not yet fixed shut.
[07:30] There is still movement possible. There is still breath in the grip.
[07:34] Letting go is not a single moment. It is not a decision you make once and then it is done. It is more like learning to trust the river, a little more today than yesterday, and a little more tomorrow than today. There will be mornings when the hand closes again. That is not failure. That is being human in a difficult world. But there is something waiting for you that pain cannot offer. Not happiness, exactly. Happiness is too light a word for what I mean. Something steadier. A kind of inner spaciousness that does not depend on circumstances being a certain way. A ground that does not shift every time memory shifts. The Buddhist tradition calls it peace, but I have always felt that word is misunderstood.
[08:17] It is not the absence of feeling. It is the presence of something larger than any single feeling.
[08:23] Something that can hold joy and grief and uncertainty all at once, without collapsing.
[08:29] That is what becomes possible when the hand opens. Not because you forced it. Not because you finally won the argument against your own pain. But because, at some point, you understood, truly understood, that you were never holding the wound to protect yourself. You were holding it because you had forgotten you were allowed to let it go. So I leave you with this. Not as instruction, but as an invitation to sit with. What would your life feel like if the thing you are carrying right now were no longer yours to carry? You do not need to answer today. Just let the question breathe.
