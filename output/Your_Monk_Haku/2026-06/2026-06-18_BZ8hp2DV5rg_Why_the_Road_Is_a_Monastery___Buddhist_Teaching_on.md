# Why the Road Is a Monastery | Buddhist Teaching on Travel and the Self

[00:00] There is a man I once knew, who spent forty years inside the same four walls, believing that stillness was the whole of the path. He meditated every morning, he chanted every evening, he never left, and when he was dying, he looked at me and said, I wonder what I missed. Those words have never left me. Most people believe that to walk a spiritual path, you must first stop walking. They imagine the holy life as a closed room, a cushion on the floor, silence on all sides, and there is truth in that. Stillness has its place, but if the Buddha himself had believed this, he would have stayed beneath that tree, and the world would never have heard his voice. The Buddha walked. For forty-five years after his awakening, he walked, village to village, river to river, season to season. He did not build a fortress and call it enlightenment. He moved, and in his moving, he taught.
[00:57] This is something we have quietly forgotten. We think of the monastery as the place where practice happens, and the road as the place where practice is interrupted. But what if that is exactly backwards? What if the road is not the interruption, but the instruction? Travel.
[01:14] Real travel. Not the kind where you carry your comfort with you everywhere you go, but the kind where you allow yourself to be genuinely displaced. [03:07] There is a teaching in Buddhism that most people hear many times before it truly lands.
[03:12] The teaching of anatta, non-self. It points to something radical, something the thinking mind resists with everything it has. It says that the self you believe yourself to be, the one with the name, the history, the opinions, the wounds, is not a fixed thing. It is a construction. A story told so many times it began to feel like a fact. The monastery can teach you this concept, but the road makes you feel it. When you travel, truly travel, your usual supports fall away one by one. Your language may not work. Your habits have no place to land. The foods you reach for are not there. The people who reflect your identity back to you are absent. And slowly, without drama, the constructed self begins to loosen.
[04:00] I remember a young monk who came to me after spending three months wandering through a country where he knew no one, and spoke none of the language. He said I kept waiting to feel lost, but instead I felt light, like I had put down something heavy I didn't know I was carrying. That lightness he described is what the Buddhists have always pointed toward. It is not emptiness in the bleak sense. It is spaciousness, the feeling of no longer being imprisoned by the weight of your own story. The ego is a creature of habit and familiarity. Feed it the same environment every day, and it grows walls, thick and convincing. Take it somewhere it cannot recognize, and those walls begin to thin. You start to see through them, and what you see on the other side is not nothing. It is something much larger than the person you thought you were.
[04:48] This is why the road has always been sacred. Not because of what you find out there, but because of what you are asked to release. The traveler who surrenders control, who allows themselves to be genuinely unknown, is practicing one of the deepest forms of Buddhist meditation there is. You do not need a teacher to assign it. The road assigns it for you.
[05:09] There is a Japanese word, mono no aware. It has no perfect translation in English, but it points to something like the bittersweet ache of things passing, the gentle grief of beauty that does not stay, the feeling you get watching cherry blossoms fall. Knowing that they're falling is part of what makes them beautiful.
[05:29] Travel is mono no aware made into a life. Buddhism teaches Anika, impermanence, as one of the three marks of existence. Everything that arises, passes. Every condition that forms, dissolves. Nothing holds its shape forever. Not a feeling. Not a relationship. Not a self. Not a world. This is not a pessimistic teaching.
[05:53] It is a liberating one. But it is very difficult to receive in the comfort of a life built carefully around permanence. Then you travel. You arrive in a city you have never seen. And it is alive and full and overwhelming. You find a small street. A particular light at a particular hour. A woman selling something fragrant from a cart. A child laughing at something you cannot see. And it is perfect. It is so perfectly itself that your chest aches with it. And then you leave. And it continues without you. The street does not mourn your departure. The light changes. The woman goes home. The child grows up. And that moment, that specific, un-repeatable moment is gone. And in its going, it teaches you everything.
[06:39] Most of us spend our lives trying to make things stay. We grip our relationships, our identities, our certainties with white knuckles. And wonder why we are exhausted. Wonder why joy, when it comes, arrives already shadowed by the fear of its ending. Travel loosens the grip. When you know you are leaving on Thursday, you stop trying to own the place. You receive it instead. You are present in a way that permanence rarely allows. You look more carefully. You breathe more slowly. You let the moment be exactly what it is, because you already know you cannot keep it. This is the practice Annika is asking of us in every moment of our lives, not just when we are on the road. Travel simply makes the lesson impossible to avoid. The monk who understands impermanence does not grieve the falling blossom. He watches it fall. He is grateful it fell while he was there to see it. And then he turns and walks on. Shunru Suzuki once said: "In the beginner's mind there are many possibilities. In the

[08:01] And that knowing, comfortable as it is, slowly closes us. We stop seeing what is actually there and begin seeing only what we expect to find. [09:13] This is Shoshin. Beginner's mind. And it is not a technique. It is a return. A return to the way you once encountered the world before habit and certainty covered it over. The great irony is this. We travel to see new places. But what we are really doing is recovering the ability to see. When you come home, if you have traveled well, you will look at your own street differently. Your own kitchen. Your own face in the mirror. The beginner's mind you found on the road does not have to stay on the road. You can bring it back. You can apply it to the life you already have. And when you do, everything becomes new again.
[09:52] This is one of the quiet gifts travel gives. It does not just show you the world. It teaches you how to look at it. There is an old tradition in Japan called the henro, a pilgrimage of 88 temples across the island of Shikoku. Pilgrims walk, sometimes for weeks, sometimes for months, carrying a staff, wearing white, moving from temple to temple in a great circle. And when they finish, they do not say they have arrived. Not because of where you go, but because of the quality of attention you bring. The henro pilgrim does not walk to collect temples the way a tourist collects photographs. He walks to be changed.
