# You Think It's Love. A Buddhist Monk Says It Might Be This Instead.

[00:00] There is a woman sitting by a window. It is late. The room is quiet, but her mind is not.
[00:06] She is waiting for a message that has not come. The person she loves said they would reach out, and they have not. And now the silence feels like a verdict. She does not know what to call what she is feeling. She calls it love. She says, "I feel this way because I love them."
[00:24] But if we sit with her for a moment, not to judge, not to fix, only to look clearly, we begin to notice something. What she is feeling is not warmth. It is not care. It is closer to fear.
[00:38] It is a tightening in the chest, a need for confirmation, a quiet panic that something she depends on might be slipping away. This is real. This is human. But this is not love.
[00:51] This is where our confusion begins. Love and attachment arrive together so often that we stop questioning whether they are the same thing. We assume that the depth of our longing is proof of the depth of our love. We think, "I suffer because of this person, therefore I must love them deeply."
[01:10] But suffering is not a measure of love. Suffering is a signal. And what it is signaling, most of the time, is that we are holding something too tightly. The Buddha taught that the root of suffering is craving, tanha, in Pali, a thirst that is never quite satisfied. We thirst for things to stay.
[01:30] We thirst for people to remain as they are, to love us as much as we need them to, to never leave, never change, never disappoint. And when reality does not cooperate, when the message does not come, when the person changes, when life moves the way life always moves, we suffer. And we call that suffering love. I am not asking you to stop feeling. That is not the teaching. The teaching is not coldness. It is not distance. Real love is not less feeling. It is a different quality of feeling entirely. One that does not grip. One that does not calculate. One that does not send you to the window at midnight, waiting for proof that you are safe. Over the next few parts of this teaching, we will look carefully at both. We will look at attachment, what it truly is, where it comes from, what it costs us. And we will look at love, what it feels like when it is clean, when it is free, when it is not secretly about ourselves. These are not simple ideas, but they are important ones,
[02:35] perhaps the most important ones. Because the woman by the window deserves to understand what she is actually feeling. Not so she can stop feeling it, but so she can begin to find her way through it.
[02:50] Hold out your hand, open it, palm facing upward. Now imagine placing something precious there, something you love, something you need, something you are afraid of losing. Now close your fist around it, hold it tightly, do not let it move. That is attachment. It begins innocently enough. We encounter something, a person, a feeling, a version of our lives that finally seems right, and something in us says, this, I want this, I want this to stay. That wanting is natural. That wanting is human. But somewhere between the wanting and the holding, a shift happens. The open hand becomes a fist. And once we are gripping, everything changes. Attachment is not affection. It is not closeness. It is a particular relationship with impermanence, one built on refusal. The attached mind looks at the nature of things, at the fact that everything changes, that everyone leaves, that nothing holds its shape forever. And it says, not this, not this one. This one stays. And it builds its entire emotional life around enforcing
[04:01] that refusal. This is why attachment is so exhausting. It is not passive. It is constant work. We monitor.
[04:09] We manage. We read between lines. We track moods. We adjust ourselves to keep the thing in place. We become architects of permanence in a world that does not permit it. And the energy we spend on this. The vigilance.
[04:23] The anxiety. The quiet dread. We do not call it what it is. We call it caring. We call it devotion.
[04:30] We say, I worry because I love. But worry is not love. Worry is attachment protecting itself.
[04:38] The woman by the window is not only waiting for a message. She is waiting for confirmation that the world is arranged the way she needs it to be. That she is loved in the way she requires. That the person she has built her sense of safety around is still in position. When the message does not come, it is not just absence she feels. It is the threat of everything unraveling. The Buddha used the word upadana, clinging, or grasping. He described it as one of the key links in the chain of suffering.
[05:10] Not because desire itself destroys us, but because when desire hardens into clinging, we place our peace entirely outside ourselves. We say, I will be okay when they call. I will be okay when they stay. I will be okay when this does not change. And peace that depends entirely on external conditions is not peace at all. It is a temporary suspension of fear. The closed fist, held long enough, begins to ache. Not from the effort of holding, but from never being open. And the tragedy is this: whatever we hold inside that fist, we cannot truly feel. We can only hold.
[05:50] Open the hand again. After the tension of the fist, there is relief in that simple gesture, the palm facing upward, relaxed, not gripping, not demanding. Something can rest there. Something can also leave. And both of those are allowed. That openness, that willingness to hold without forcing, is closer to love than anything we have discussed so far. Love, in its cleaner form, does not need the other person to stay in order to function. It does not require confirmation, or proof, or reciprocation at a specific volume. It is not a transaction where we give in order to receive. It is closer to warmth in the way that sunlight is warm. Not because it demands something from what it touches, but simply because that is its nature. This can sound cold if you hear it the wrong way. As though I am saying love does not care. But it is the opposite. Love cares deeply, perhaps more deeply than attachment ever could, because it sees the other person clearly. Attachment does not see clearly. Attachment sees a reflection of
[07:01] its own needs. It looks at another person and sees, source of safety, source of validation, source of proof that I am lovable. It is looking, but it is not truly seeing. Love looks and sees a person, separate, whole, and not belonging to you. This is what the great teacher Tish Nhat Hanh meant when he wrote that the most precious gift you can give someone is your true presence. Not your need. Not your fear dressed as devotion. Your actual attention, given freely, without an agenda underneath it. The woman by the window, if she were to love without attachment, would still feel warmth toward this person. She would still care how they were doing, still hope for their well-being, still feel tenderness when she thought of them. But she would not be sitting in the dark, measuring her own worth by the silence of her phone. Because her love would not be structured around what she receives in return. It would be its own quiet thing, complete in itself. This kind of love is not easy. I will not pretend it is.
[08:05] It asks something significant from us. It asks us to locate our sense of safety inside ourselves rather than inside another person. It asks us to release the fantasy that someone else, if they love us correctly and consistently, will finally make us feel whole.
[08:22] That fantasy is one of the most painful ones we carry. And love, real love, asks us to lay it down.
[08:30] The open hand can feel more exposed than the closed fist. There is vulnerability in not gripping.
[08:37] There is risk in allowing things to be what they are, rather than what we need them to be.
[08:42] But inside that vulnerability is something the fist never allows: actual contact. When the hand is open, you can truly feel what rests there. And what rests there can truly feel you.
[08:54] Let me tell you about a father and his daughter. He loved her from the moment she was born. That love was real, unambiguous, total, the way early love between a parent and child often is.
[09:09] He would have done anything for her. In many ways, he did. He built a life around her safety, her comfort, her future. Every decision carried her somewhere inside it.
[09:21] But as she grew older, something in his love began to calcify. When she chose friends he did not approve of, he felt it as a wound. When she disagreed with his values he experienced it as betrayal. When she moved away to build her own life, he was unable to fully celebrate it. What he felt instead was a grief he did not understand. And underneath the grief, a bitterness he did not want to name. He had given so much. Why was she not staying closer? He would not have called this attachment. He would have called it love. He would have said, "I only want what is best for her." And part of him would have meant it. But another part, the part we are examining now, had quietly turned love into a contract, one she never signed. This is the moment love becomes a cage. Not through cruelty, but through need. The father did not want to trap his daughter. He wanted to keep what he loved, in the arrangement he loved it in. But a person is not an arrangement. A person is always becoming, always moving, always a little further than the
[10:29] last place you held them. We do this in romantic love as well. We meet someone, we open to them, and somewhere along the way, we begin to design them. We begin to need them to be a certain way. To show up in the ways that make us feel safe. To remain the version of themselves we first fell in love with.
[10:48] And when they change, which they will, because people always do, we feel wronged. We call it falling out of love. But often what we mean is, "They stopped fitting the shape I needed them to hold."
[11:02] The cage is not always visible. It is often built from the most sincere materials. Worry, sacrifice, devotion. The person inside it often cannot name what is wrong for a very long time. They only feel, gradually, that they cannot quite breathe. That there is a script they are supposed to follow. That love, here, has conditions, even if those conditions are never spoken aloud. The woman by the window, if she is not careful, will one day become the one building cages. Because unexamined attachment teaches itself to the next relationship, and the one after that. The fear underneath it does not dissolve on its own. But awareness changes things. When we can see the fist, we can choose, slowly, to open it. Not because we love less, but because we are beginning to understand what love actually asks of us.
[11:56] There is still a woman by a window. But tonight is different. The silence is the same. The phone has not lit up. The message has not come. The facts of the situation are unchanged, and yet something in her has shifted, just slightly. She is sitting with the silence rather than fighting it. She is not measuring herself against it. She notices the tightness in her chest, and she does not immediately try to resolve it by reaching for her phone, or by composing arguments in her mind, or by cycling through every possible reason the silence might mean something terrible. She simply sits. She breathes. She notices that she is still here, still whole, even inside the uncertainty. This is not detachment. I want to be clear about that. Detachment is a kind of armor, a way of not feeling in order to not suffer. What I am describing is something more difficult and more alive than that. It is the practice of remaining fully present with our feelings, fully connected to the people we love. While releasing our grip on the
[12:59] outcomes, it is loving with an open hand. The Buddhist tradition does not ask us to love less. It asks us to love more cleanly, to disentangle love from fear, to notice when our care for another person has quietly become about managing our own anxiety, to ask ourselves honestly, "Am I loving this person, or am I loving the way this person makes me feel? Am I wishing for their freedom and flourishing, or am I wishing for them to stay in the position that makes me feel safe?" These are not comfortable questions, but they are clarifying ones. The father and his daughter, if he were willing to look honestly at what happened between them, might find that there is still time, not to undo the years, but to begin again with a different understanding, to love her without the contract, to find, perhaps for the first time, that loving someone without needing them to behave in a particular way is not a loss, it is a relief, for both of them. Open-handed love is not passive, it is not indifferent, it is one of the most active
[14:05] and demanding practices a human being can undertake. It requires us to build inside ourselves a source of steadiness that does not depend entirely on what others do or do not give us. It requires us to grieve the fantasy that another person, if they love us well enough, will finally complete us, and it requires us to show up again and again for the people we love, not because of what they give us in return, but because love, when it is clean, does not need a reason. The woman closes her eyes. She thinks of the person she loves, not what she needs from them, not whether they will call, but simply them. As a person, living their life, carrying their own weight, she wishes them well, genuinely, quietly, without agenda.
[14:51] That small wish, offered freely into the silence, is closer to love than anything she has felt all night.
