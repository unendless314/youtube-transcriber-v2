# How to Stop Feeling Jealous When Others Succeed

[00:00] You smiled. Then it stung. That's not bad character. When a close friend shares their breakthrough, the promotion you hoped for, the relationship you've prayed for, the life you have secretly dreamed of, you say, congratulations, while a sharp, cold knot twists in your chest.
[00:18] And immediately, guilt follows. What kind of toxic friend am I that I can't just be purely happy for them? In Zen, we do not judge the mind's initial reflex. Envy is not a moral failure.
[00:30] It is pure information. That sting you feel isn't hatred for your friend. It is your own unexpressed desire holding up a mirror. Envy doesn't mean you want to take anything away from them.
[00:42] It simply names what your soul is starving for in your own life. It tells you precisely what you value, what you yearn for, and where your own path is calling you to grow. Buddhist psychology offers a profound remedy called mudita, the practice of sympathetic joy or rejoicing in the good fortune of others. Mudita teaches us that another person's abundance is not a lottery where their win steals your ticket. The universe is not a finite room with limited light. Their success does not diminish your potential. It merely proves that what you seek is possible. When you feel that familiar pang, stop fighting yourself. Do not add a second arrow of shame to the first wound of comparison. Breathe in, acknowledge the desire underneath the envy without judgment, and wish your friend well from the depths of your heart. Their victory is theirs to celebrate. Your path is yours to walk.
[01:42] Return your focus to your own soil and tend to your own garden.
