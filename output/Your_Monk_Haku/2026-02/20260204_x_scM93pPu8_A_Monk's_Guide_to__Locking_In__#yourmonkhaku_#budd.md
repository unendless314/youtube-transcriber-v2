# A Monk's Guide to "Locking In" #yourmonkhaku #buddhism #motivation #mindfulness #meditation

[00:00] You have been running from a ghost. You call it distraction. You call it a lack of discipline.
 Or perhaps you simply call it your personality. You believe that to lock in is a matter of adding
 something to your life, a better desk, a more rigorous schedule. But look at the poet Victor
 Hugo. In the winter of 1830, he did not add. He subtracted. He stripped himself of his very
 dignity, locking his clothes away and draping himself in a grey shawl, a monk's robe of his
 own making. He turned his home into a cell. He understood a truth that your modern world
 tries to hide from you. The mind will only settle when it is cornered. You have lived
 your life with the doors wide open, wondering why your spirit constantly wanders into the
 streets of Paris. You have given yourself too many exits. To truly lock in is not an act
 of will, it is an act of siege. It is the recognition that as long as there is an alternative to the
 work, the ego will find a way to take it. Hugo did not finish his masterpiece, because he was
 more virtuous than you. He finished it, because he made it impossible to do anything else. He sat
 half-naked in the cold, with nothing but a bottle of ink and the silence. He surrendered to the
 manuscript. You think you need more motivation, but what you actually need is a smaller world.
 You need to create a space where the you who procrastinates has nowhere to hide. There is a
 great violence you commit against yourself every day, and you call it multitasking. You have been
 told that being productive means spinning many plates, holding many threads, being everywhere at
 once. But in the realm of the spirit, there is no such thing as parallel processing. There is only the
 frantic jumping of a monkey from branch to branch, losing its breath with every leap. Each time you
 switch your gaze from the task to the screen, from the deep work to the trivial notification, you pay a
 tax in blood. You leave a piece of your presence behind. The Buddha spoke of one-pointedness, a state
 where the observer and the observed become one. When you try to do everything, you end up being nowhere.
 This macro-multitasking is a sickness of the modern age. You try to build a business while mastering a
 craft, while fixing your health, while maintaining a social image. You are spreading your light so thin
 that it can no longer burn through the paper. To lock in is to choose one flame. It is the courage to
 let the other plates fall and shatter. You fear that if you focus on only one thing, you will miss out on
 the world. But the irony is that by trying to hold the whole world, you never truly touch a single part
 of it. Depth is the only place where transformation happens. Consider the concept of the context window.
 When you immerse yourself deeply in a single pursuit, the mind begins to expand. It begins to pull in
 threads of information from the furthest reaches of your experience and weave them together. You see this
 in the obsessed scholar or the dedicated craftsman. They are not smarter than you. They are simply
 deeper. They have stayed in the room long enough for the dust to settle and the patterns to emerge. When you
 jump from task to task, your context window is narrow, cramped and shallow. You are playing a game of surface
 level. You wonder why you lack insights or breakthroughs. It is because you do not stay with the question long enough for it to
 answer you. To lock in is to allow a single subject to climb inside you and wear you like a garment. It is to
 stay in the silence until the silence begins to speak. This is the secret of the parasite of obsession. You
 must let the work inhabit you. If you spend ninety days on a single goal, you will achieve more than in two years of
 half-hearted striving. Why? Because the mind requires time to warm the engine of creation. You cannot reach the

 the air gets thin. We must speak of the lie you tell yourself about your laziness. You think you
 procrastinate because you are weak-willed. You think you are fixed in this state of stagnation. But look
 closer. Procrastination is not a defect of character. It is armor. It is a self-protection
 strategy wearing a mask. You delay the work because you are terrified of what the work will say about you.
 If you try your absolute best and you fail, then you have no excuses left. You are revealed as not
 enough. So you choose the safety of the hypothetical. You tell yourself, I could have been great if I had
 tried. This is the psychological insurance policy of the ego. It is the safety blanket that keeps you
 warm while your potential freezes to death. By procrastinating, you choose a private, deniable
 failure over a public, honest effort. You would rather be a genius who didn't try than a beginner who
 failed. But this armor is heavy. It is suffocating you. Every time you hide from the task,
 you are certifying your failure in private to avoid the mere possibility of it in public. You are choosing
 the fake safety of a dream over the messy, beautiful reality of being a human who struggles.
 Who is the one who is afraid to fail? It is the I that you have built out of your achievements and the
 opinions of others. You have tied your very right to exist to your productivity. This is why a blank page
 feels like a threat to your life. You are wrestling with your self-worth, not your schedule. You think that if
 the work is not perfect, you are not perfect. But the Buddha saw through this phantom. He saw that the
 self you are trying to protect is a construction, a collection of stories and memories that has no solid
 core. When you lock in, you are not just working, you are risking your identity. The self-improvement industry

 higher. It makes the fear of failing feel like the fear of dying. To truly lock in, you must realize
 that the work you do is not you. It is simply a dance of causes and conditions. If the dance is clumsy
 today, it does not mean the dancer is broken. The dancer is just a flow of experience. When you
 stop trying to protect a ghost, you will find that you have a great deal more energy to actually do the
 work. There is a comfort in the unlived life. In your mind, you are a master. You are the author of a
 book. The founder of a thriving temple. The paragon of health. As long as you do not begin, this image
 remains intact. This is the trap of hypothetical excellence. It is a decision to live in theory
 rather than in practice. You congratulate yourself for your caution and your high standards. But these
 are merely the bars of your cage. You are choosing the version of you who could have done great things
 over the version of you who actually might. In the monastery, we say that the only bad meditation
 is the one that didn't happen. The same is true for your work. A messy, flawed, embarrassing first
 draft is infinitely more holy than a perfect idea that remains in your head. The perfect idea is dead.

 the only true loss. The loss of the experience itself. You are staying intact while your dreams
 starve. You must be willing to break the image of who you think you are to become who you actually
 are. The antidote to your fear is not confidence. Confidence is often just the ego pretending it
 knows the outcome. The true antidote is surrender. It is the willingness to be seen beginning. It is the
 acceptance of the embarrassment of being a novice. You want to skip the awkwardness. You want to be the

 becomes easy. The hardest part of the work is the identity shift, moving from someone who protects
 their image to someone who risks it. This is a form of spiritual death, and it is the only way to be
 reborn into mastery. You don't need courage to begin. You just need the willingness to be seen as you are,
