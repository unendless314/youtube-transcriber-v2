# 10 Buddhist Lessons I Learned in Paris (This Changed How I See Everything)

[00:00] I have walked the streets of one of the most beautiful cities in the world, not as a tourist chasing landmarks, not as a man with a full schedule and a camera ready, but as a monk slowly, quietly, with open hands and no agenda, and what Paris taught me had nothing to do with Paris. It taught me about impermanence, about the way we grasp at beauty and lose it in the grasping, about the sacred that hides inside ordinary cafes and rainy afternoons and cobblestone streets where you have no idea where you are going, about the kind of seeing that is only possible when you stop trying to possess what you see. In this sermon, I will share ten lessons that came to me not in meditation, not in scripture, but in the streets of a foreign city where I did not speak the language and did not know the way, each one arrived quietly, the way the most important teachings always do. [01:30] Part 1. The city teaches you to slow down.
[01:34] I did not expect Paris to be a teacher of stillness. I arrived with tired feet before I had even walked a single street, carrying the weight of a schedule, a list of places I was told I must see, and yet, the city had other plans for me. On the first morning, I sat beside the Seine. I had intended to cross it quickly, to move on to the next destination on my list. But something stopped me. Perhaps it was the light on the water. Perhaps it was the old man feeding pigeons without any urgency, as though he had nowhere else to be and had made peace with that. I watched him for a long time. I forgot for a moment that I was a traveller with places to go. In the Zen tradition, we speak of a concept called Shoshin, beginner's mind. It is the practice of approaching each moment as if for the first time, without the noise of expectation or the rush of agenda. Paris, in its unhurried mornings and its long evening meals, embodies this without knowing it. The French do not eat quickly. They do
[02:37] not walk with the frantic energy I have seen in other cities. They seem to understand, perhaps instinctively, that life is not improved by acceleration. I thought of how many mornings I had rushed through my own temple duties. The incense, the bell, the prayers, completing them with my body while my mind was already somewhere else. Paris reminded me that speed is not a virtue, that presence is the only offering worth giving. The city did not ask me to hurry, and in that, it gave me something I had forgotten I needed.
[03:09] Art 2. Beauty is everywhere, but only the present eye can see it. There is a painting in the Musée d'Orsay that stopped me completely. I do not say this lightly. I have sat in meditation for many hours without moving.
[03:24] But this was different. I was stopped not by discipline, but by something I was not prepared for. The colours, the light captured in brush strokes, the way a moment in time had been held still for over a century. It asked nothing of me except that I look. And I realised, standing there among the crowd, that most of the people passing by were not seeing it. They were photographing it.
[03:49] There is a difference. To photograph something is to say, I will see this later. To truly look is to say, I am here now, and this is enough. The Buddhist teachings speak often of the veil of habit.
[04:02] We stop seeing the things we encounter regularly, because familiarity creates a kind of blindness.
[04:08] The sunrise becomes background. The face of someone we love becomes furniture. We look without seeing, because our minds are already moving toward the next thing. Paris has a way of breaking that veil, if you allow it. A doorway carved with flowers you would never notice in a hurry. A courtyard hiding behind an ordinary gate. Light falling through a narrow street at an angle that exists for perhaps ten minutes a day. These things do not announce themselves. They wait for the one who is paying attention. I have returned to my temple and looked at it differently since. The moss on the stone steps.
[04:43] The particular sound the wooden floor makes in winter. Beauty did not travel back with me in my bag.
[04:49] It was always here. I had simply forgotten to look.
[04:54] Part 3. Being a stranger is a gift. I do not speak French. I want to be clear about this, because it matters to what I am about to share. I walked through Paris as a man who could not read most signs, could not follow most conversations, could not rely on the comfort of language to navigate the world around him, and something unexpected happened as a result. I became very quiet inside.
[05:20] When you cannot fill your mind with the meaning of words around you, you begin to notice everything else.
[05:25] The way a shopkeeper arranges fruit with the care of someone who considers it a practice.
[05:30] The way two elderly women greet each other on the street, holding both hands, pressing cheeks, as if the meeting itself is a small ceremony. The way a child looks at a puddle, not as an obstacle, but as a mirror of the sky. In Zen, there is a teaching about the usefulness of emptiness.
[05:48] A cup is only useful because of the space inside it. A room is only useful because of the empty space it contains. When I could not understand the language around me, I became in a sense an empty cup, and the world filled me in ways it cannot, when I am already full of words and interpretation.
[06:06] We are often afraid of being strangers. We seek familiarity, belonging, the comfort of being known and understood. But there is a freedom in not being known. No one in Paris had any expectation of me. No one knew my role, my history, my habits. I was simply a man walking slowly, looking carefully.
[06:28] It is a rare condition, and I found it deeply nourishing. Do not always rush back to the familiar.
[06:34] The stranger you become, the more clearly you sometimes see.
[06:38] Part 4. Every language is a different way of thinking.
[06:42] There was a young woman at a small cafe near the Luxembourg Gardens who tried to explain something to me about the coffee she was serving. Her English was limited. My French was non-existent.
[06:53] And yet we spent several minutes in genuine communication, through gesture, through expression, through the universal grammar of two people who actually want to understand each other.
[07:03] When I finally tasted the coffee and nodded slowly, she laughed with a satisfaction that needed no translation. I thought about that exchange for the rest of the day.
[07:13] Language is not simply a tool for transferring information. It is a structure through which a people understand reality. The French have words for feelings and conditions that do not exist in English or in Japanese, and Japanese holds concepts that cannot be fully carried into French without losing something essential in the crossing. Each language is not merely a different sound for the same thing. It is a different way of carving up the world. This humbled me deeply.
[07:40] I have spent my life studying Buddhist texts, many of which were first written in Sanskrit or Pali, then carried into Chinese, then into Japanese, then into English. At every crossing, something shifts. A word that holds an entire philosophy in one language becomes a pale approximation in another. This is not failure. This is the nature of all things, meaning, like water takes the shape of the vessel that holds it. What Paris taught me through its language is this. Your way of seeing is not the only way of seeing. Your framework is not the framework. There are entire worlds of understanding living inside languages you will never speak. Approach that not with frustration, but with reverence. The wise man is not the one who knows many languages. He is the one who knows that every language knows something he does not.
[08:31] Part 5. Art does not explain itself. And neither should you. I stood in front of a sculpture for a long time at the Rodin Museum. It was not the most famous piece in the garden. It was smaller, set slightly apart, and most visitors walked past it without stopping. I could not tell you precisely what it depicted. I could not tell you what the artist intended. And yet something in it reached across the silence, and touched a place in me that I do not have words for. I did not need an explanation. The experience was the explanation.
[09:05] We live in an age that is deeply uncomfortable with ambiguity. We want to know what things mean. We want artists to explain their work. Teachers to explain their methods. People to explain their choices. We have developed an enormous appetite for justification. As though nothing has value until it has been narrated and labelled and filed away in the correct category of understanding. But the greatest things resist this. A piece of music that moves you to tears does not do so because you understand its theory. A landscape that stops your breath does not wait for you to identify its geological features. And a life lived with integrity and compassion does not require a detailed explanation to be recognized as good.
[09:46] I have met many people who exhaust themselves explaining who they are. They perform their identity constantly, offering justifications for their choices, their beliefs, their path. It is an exhausting way to live, and it often means they are trying to convince themselves as much as anyone else.
[10:04] The sculpture did not explain itself. It simply was. And in being fully what it was, without apology or annotation, it communicated everything. Love like that. Be so fully yourself that explanation becomes unnecessary.
[10:18] Part 6. Getting lost is not failure. On my third day in Paris, I made a wrong turn somewhere behind the Marais district, and walked for nearly an hour without recognizing anything around me. I had no map open, my phone was low on power, and the streets curved in ways that gave me no reliable sense of direction.
[10:38] By any conventional measure, I was lost. It was one of the finest hours of the entire journey.
[10:45] I found a courtyard where a man was repairing a bicycle in complete silence. I passed a bookshop so narrow that two people could not browse it at the same time. I discovered a small square with a single tree in the center, and three old men playing cards beneath it, unbothered by the rest of the world entirely. None of these things were in any guide. None of them were recommended to me. I found them only because I did not know where I was going. We speak in Buddhist teaching about the danger of the fixed path. When we are too attached to arriving at a predetermined destination, we close ourselves to everything that exists outside that narrow corridor. We move through life with our eyes forward, and our plans firm, and we miss the courtyard, the bookshop, the three old men, the single tree.
[11:30] Getting lost, in the deepest sense, is not a failure of navigation. It is a temporary release from the tyranny of the known. It places you in direct contact with the present moment because you have no choice. You cannot think about where you are going. You can only attend to where you are. I have tried to carry this practice back into my daily life, to occasionally release the plan, to take the unfamiliar road, to trust that what I find there has value, even if it was not what I was looking for. The destination will wait, or it will not. Either way, you will have lived the walk.
[12:09] Part 7. The café and the temple are not so different. I sat in a Parisian café on a rainy afternoon, and I noticed something that I could not immediately name. There was a quality to the atmosphere that felt in some way I could not justify, familiar. Not familiar in the way of having been there before.
[12:27] Familiar in the way of recognizing something essential. It took me the length of a full cup of coffee to understand what it was. People came to this café not only for coffee. They came to sit with their thoughts.
[12:40] They came to be alone without being lonely. They came to mark the passing of an hour with something small and warm held between their hands. They came, in short, for the same reasons people come to a temple.
[12:52] We tend to think of sacred space as something formally designated, a building with the correct architecture, the correct symbols, the correct history. We believe that the sacred requires permission, requires ceremony, requires the right conditions before it can be entered. But this is a misunderstanding that has caused a great deal of unnecessary suffering. The sacred is not a location. It is a quality of attention. Wherever a human being sits quietly and turns inward, even briefly, even imperfectly, something temple-like is happening. The café on a rainy afternoon, the park bench at dusk, the kitchen before the rest of the house has woken. These are all temples, if the one sitting in them is truly present. The monk does not own stillness. The temple does not manufacture peace. They are simply invitations. The same invitation exists in a Parisian café, in the smell of coffee and rain on stone, if you are willing to accept it. Do not wait for the correct conditions to practice presence.
[13:54] The conditions are always correct. Only the willingness is sometimes missing.
[13:59] Part 8: Impermanence lives in every cobblestone. Paris is a very old city. This is something you know before you arrive, but it is something different to feel it beneath your feet. Walking on streets that have been walked for centuries, past buildings that have witnessed revolutions and occupations and celebrations and ordinary Tuesdays repeated 10,000 times, there is a weight to it that no photograph can prepare you for.
[14:26] And yet, for all its age, Paris is also constantly disappearing. The light changes every hour. The market that fills a street in the morning is gone by afternoon. The conversation at the next table rises and falls and ends and those people leave and are replaced by others who will also leave. The city that existed at seven in the morning is not the city that exists at seven in the evening. It has changed in ways both visible and invisible. The Buddhist concept of Anika, Impermanence, is sometimes taught as a sombre truth, a reminder of loss. But I have come to understand it differently. Impermanence is not only the announcement of ending. It is also the guarantee of freshness. Because nothing is fixed, everything remains alive.
[15:11] Because the moment cannot be held, it must be fully inhabited while it exists.
[15:16] The cobblestones of Paris have been worn smooth by millions of footsteps. Each one of those people believed, as we all do, that their concerns were permanent, their griefs were permanent, their joys were permanent. None of it was, all of it passed. And the stone remains, worn a little smoother, indifferent and patient. I found this not depressing but deeply relieving. My worries too will wear smooth with time. My attachments too will loosen. What feels permanent today is already in the process of passing. Walk gently on the old stones. And hold everything you carry a little more lightly.
[15:54] Part 9. You cannot carry Paris home. And that is the lesson.
[15:59] On my last evening in Paris, I walked slowly through streets I had come to know over the previous days.
[16:06] I was already in some part of my mind trying to preserve it, trying to memorize the particular quality of the light, the smell of the bakeries still warm in the early evening, the sound of the city settling into its quieter nighttime rhythm. I wanted to take it with me. I wanted to own the experience in some permanent way. And I noticed, as I walked, that this wanting was already pulling me out of the very experience I was trying to preserve. In reaching for Paris, I was losing Paris. The grasping itself was the obstacle. This is one of the oldest teachings in Buddhist practice, and Paris delivered it to me once more with great patience. Attachment is not love. Clinging is not appreciation. When we try to freeze an experience to capture and possess it, we are no longer inside it. We are already standing outside it, pressing our hands against the glass. The memories I carry from Paris are not Paris.
[17:02] They are echoes. Reflections in still water that shift the moment you reach for them. The real Paris existed only in those moments when I was fully inside them. Not thinking about how to keep them. Not photographing them for later. Not narrating them to some imagined future version of myself. This is true of every beautiful thing. The conversation that moves you. The meal shared with someone you love. The moment of unexpected grace that arrives without warning. You cannot carry any of it home. You can only be present while it is happening and release it when it ends. The letting go is not the loss. The letting go is the practice. And the practice done with an open hand and a willing heart is its own kind of joy.
[17:45] Part 10 Travel does not change the world. It changes the one who travels.
[17:51] I returned to my temple on a Tuesday morning. The stone steps were exactly as I had left them.
[17:58] The moss in the same places. The bell waiting in its usual silence. The garden neither welcoming nor indifferent. Simply continuing, as gardens do, in their slow and patient work of growing. Nothing had changed while I was gone. And yet I stood at the gate for a long time before entering. Because I understood that I was not quite the same person who had left. This is the quiet secret of travel that no guidebook will tell you. You do not go to a place to change the place. The place does not need you.
[18:28] Paris existed for centuries before you arrived, and will continue for centuries after you leave, largely undisturbed by your presence. You go to a place to be changed by it. If you are willing. If you remain open. If you do not spend the entire journey looking for confirmation of what you already believe.
[18:47] The monk who leaves his temple and returns is not the same monk. Not because he has acquired new information. New photographs. New stories for conversation. But because he has been quietly rearranged by encounters he did not plan. By beauty he did not expect. By moments of stillness found in the most unlikely places beside a river. In front of a sculpture. In a cafe on a rainy afternoon. On a cobblestone street where he was completely and peacefully lost. In the Zen tradition. There is a famous image of the traveller returning home. After the long journey. After all the seeking and finding and losing and discovering. He returns to the ordinary. The same gate. The same steps. The same bell. But now he sees them clearly. Perhaps for the first time. That is what Paris gave me. Not a new world. A new pair of eyes with which to see the one I already live in. Go. Get lost. Come home changed.
[19:43] That is enough. That is everything.
[19:45] To conclude. Paris did not ask anything of me. It simply existed. In all its beauty and noise and ancient stillness. And allowed me to walk through it. Whatever I received.
