# The Career Advice You Grew Up With Is Dead in the Age of AI

[00:00] Hello world, I'm an unemployed ex-big tech software engineer with 25 years of experience in the tech industry. So with each passing week we see more and more rounds of mass layoffs in tech with tens of thousands of professionals being laid off with AI given as a stated reason. And so it begs the question, how are people going to survive and thrive in the age of AI? As a parent of two kids this question has been weighing heavily on my mind. Now that I got some time on my hands I've been able to dig into this topic a bit and have some thoughts to share. Before we drill down the strategies that will work let's first talk about the strategies that most likely will not work. So the first one is the paradigm of going to a university, getting a set of knowledge, and then through very minor retraining and updates that knowledge expecting to get a career that will be high paying and lasts for several decades. This paradigm in my humble opinion is almost certainly doomed. Think about this:
[01:07] you take a young person and you have him spend four years taking on god knows how much debt to learn this basket of facts and processes. Then he's expected to mostly regurgitate variations of these facts and processes over the years of his early to mid career. So in effect it's almost like college is creating this vastly inferior but exponentially more expensive version of a large language model. So employers would be naturally averse to hiring fresh college graduates for good wages in the age of AI. Now don't get me wrong, higher education had utility in the past and it may yet have utility in the AI age. In ancient Greece, for example, people attended academies in order to broaden their horizons and to learn how to learn.
[02:07] During the Victorian period, attending college was a very good way of signaling your status as a member of the elite class. But with all that said, the idea that higher education is like some kind of a conveyor belt where people get on, learn their knowledge, and then get off with a high paying stable career and they're waiting for them, that idea is just done. Now another paradigm is to pursue a career in a stable, boring, safe, non-tech, white collar field. Think accounting, insurance, administration, things like that.
[02:51] This approach, in my opinion, is highly unlikely to be viable. Why is that? Well, let's run a little thought experiment. Think of a white collar profession, not as this entire profession, but as a sequence of activities and workflows. For each of these workflows, we need to ask ourselves two questions. One, is this workflow repetitive? Has it been done before in some form, in some variation in the past? Two, does the work happen on a computer? If the answer to these two questions are both yes, it is highly likely for AI to replace this workflow. So professions where most of the work done meets these two criteria? These jobs are not going to get replaced one for one by AI, but rather parts and components of the job would get dissolved by AI. Kind of like an ice cube melting under the sun, where you eventually end up with dramatically fewer numbers of people doing the same amount of work. So what about going into the trades? Now, there is a lot of advice circulating out there saying that the skilled trades is a short path
[04:15] to a stable, well-paying profession in the age of AI. However, I see two major challenges to this approach: a near-term challenge and a medium-term challenge. The near-term challenge is that now you have a huge number of people looking for stable, well-paying work. You got tons of laid-off, white-collar professionals looking for jobs. You have your junior college graduates that can't get a job in the first place.
[04:45] You have tons of gig workers looking to earn more income to survive. So you got all these people looking for good-paying work, and a good portion of them are going to try to get into the trades. When we look at the hard numbers, we're talking about something like 50 times the number of people entering into trade schools when compared to the number of new trade jobs that are being created. This will create intense competition and downward pressure on trade job wages in the immediate near-term. Now, the medium-term challenge is the adoption of general-purpose robots. The general-purpose humanoid robots of today still have some issues that need to be worked out. Issues like battery life. Issues like engineering hands that are durable, dexterous, and robust. Issues like collecting the huge volumes of training data necessary to build models that can account for all the messy situations that happens in the real world when a robot is executing a task. But look, all of these are solvable engineering problems. None of this
[06:09] requires some kind of theoretical breakthrough to achieve. Because of this, most analysts out there believe that the widespread adoption of general-purpose humanoid robots in America would happen by the mid-2030s.
[06:25] So that's less than a decade away. Hardly enough time to establish a career in the trades, even if you started today. So if these strategies are unlikely to work, then what will? With that, I like to share some coping strategies that might just work for people, at least for a while.
[06:46] Let's get into it. One strategy is to master the AI tools. AI tools are proliferating across many professional disciplines. You got things like cloud code for software engineering, cloud design for UI/UX, cloud legal for legal, co-work for accounting, and so on. If you already have a job in one of these professions, now is the time to learn these AI tools and maximize your output with them. In software engineering for example, now it is not only possible but quite repeatable for a skilled software engineer to 5x or even 10x their productivity when using AI tools. Remember that for the foreseeable future, AI isn't going to be replacing people. It's going to be other people using AI that replaces people.
[07:43] So with the mastery of AI tools, you can be one of those people. Another strategy is to disrupt your own job with AI. If you disrupt yourself, the outcome will usually be much better for you than if someone else disrupts your job. How do you disrupt your own job? Well, think of all the processes and workflows that you do on a day-to-day basis. Are any of these workflows repetitive? If it is, then potentially AI can do some, if not all of that work. The tools are so good now that you can literally spin up agents to do most repetitive cognitive work. Work like analyzing and answering emails, doing basic research, creating reports. And you can do this usually without writing a single line of code.
[08:41] The impact of this self disruption is twofold. The first is that you're able to do your job better, securing your work more. The second impact is that you free up massive amounts of your own time. Time that you can use to figure out other plans, ideas, and sources of income. Another strategy is to become very good in professions where the focus is fundamentally on working with humans. Work that requires building trust and managing interpersonal relationships. Think areas like healthcare, human services, leadership, and so on. Work that fundamentally requires nuanced human-to-human interactions.
[09:32] That may be something that AI cannot replace for the foreseeable future. So, another viable strategy is to focus on supporting the AI agents. Now, you can think of an AI agent as nothing more than the software program that feeds prompts to a large language model in a controlled way in order to do work.
[09:59] Nowadays, agents can do all sorts of cognitive labor. Work like computer programming, writing marketing copy, it can make decisions, it can buy and sell things online, and it does all of this on behalf of humans.
[10:17] So, with each passing week, more and more AI agents are coming online. And with that agentic growth, there is an entire supporting ecosystem that's being built to support these AI agents.
[10:32] This ecosystem allows agents to discover new information. It allows agents to interact with digital and real-world systems. And of course, it allows agents to do commerce and transactions.
[10:48] The build-out of this agentic support ecosystem is a massive undertaking. It's a huge opportunity that will require quite a few people and many years to fully flesh out. Now, one very viable strategy is to disrupt existing business models with AI. The idea here is to reimagine how an existing, already profitable business can be either duplicated or improved by AI. Perhaps the business model can just be duplicated with AI agents and offered for sale at a much lower price. Perhaps the underlying problem that this business model is trying to solve could be improved to be better, faster, more efficient with AI.
[11:37] The point I'm making here is that almost any current existing digital business models that are making money, it can be disrupted with this approach. And the owners of these existing businesses would naturally be averse to disrupting their own business models. Why is that? Because these business models are currently making the money. And people usually don't want to kill the goose that lays golden eggs.
[12:08] By the way, this kind of crab-like mentality is nothing new. You see companies behave this way time and time again, well before AIs and large language models came onto the scene. Think about a company like Kodak and how they reacted to the rise of digital photography. Or think about Blockbuster and how they reacted to the rise of streaming services. So this kind of defensive thinking is why we see so many small startups and even solo entrepreneurs pop up with new digital products that eat away at the market share of these incumbent existing businesses. And in doing so, it's making these new entrepreneurs huge, crazy amounts of monthly reoccurring revenue. Now, I want to be blunt. This strategy is hard, guys. It requires that you put yourself out there and actively disrupt something that already exists. And so the chance of failure will naturally be high. But the upside here is that if you're successful, the result could be a huge life changing sum of money. But the window of opportunity here is quite limited. Because it's relatively easy
[13:30] to reproduce something that you already know and you know already makes money. So everybody would be looking into doing this. Now, the last strategy is to create entirely new business models with AI. This is a business model that did not exist and perhaps could not have existed before AI technology arrived on the scene. A concrete example of this approach is neural machine translation. We see this technology all the time with things like YouTube auto dubbing, for example, right? Where a person in a video speaks in one language and in near real time, it's translated into all of these other languages while perfectly capturing the speakers tone, pace and nuance. This capability and business model simply could not have existed before the arrival of deep learning neural networks. How many such business models are out there? Either businesses that nobody has thought of yet, or business models that people have thought of in the past, But abandoned because it was too cognitively expensive to make it real because AI just wasn't available at that time.
[14:52] This is kind of like being on the ground floor when the internet was being built up.
