My name is Louis, and this is the Coin Bureau. Privacy wasn't an afterthought in crypto. It was, at least originally, the whole point. [01:44] The Cypherpunks built digital cash for one reason. They wanted money nobody could watch. And in 2008, Satoshi Nakamoto's white paper carried that idea forward, with an entire section titled Privacy.
[01:58] But the plan there was never true privacy. Instead, it was pseudonymity. Your name wasn't on the ledger, but every transaction was for anyone to read. And firms like Chainalysis and Elliptic turned linking those addresses to real people into a business. Tracing work around the Silk Road takedown and the 2016 Bitfinex hack showed that once coins touched an exchange that knew your identity, the trail led straight back to you. So, for exchanges and regulators, the glass ledger became a feature rather than a flaw.
[02:33] Now, Monero and Zcash were built as the answer to this. Coins designed to hide what Bitcoin exposed.
[02:40] But the industry's response was to show them the door. Bittrex delisted them in 2021. OKEx and Binance followed with sweeping regional delistings through 2023 and 2024. [03:02] So, the industry made a deal with itself. If crypto wanted to go mainstream, it would have to accept being fully transparent. But what happens when a government decides the privacy tools themselves are the problem? Well, in August 2022, the US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control, or OFAC, sanctioned Tornado Cash. Tornado Cash was a mixer, a set of smart contracts that pooled funds together so they couldn't be traced back to their source. And this was the first time a government had sanctioned software itself rather than a person. A couple of days later, the fight moved from the code to the people who wrote it. Dutch authorities arrested Tornado Cash developer Alexei Pertsev in Amsterdam. And in May 2024, a Dutch court convicted him of money laundering and sentenced him to 64 months. He was released under electronic monitoring in February 2025 while he appeals. And that appeal is still pending. Then in the US, Tornado Cash co-founder Roman Storm was indicted in 2023. In August 2025,
[04:09] a jury convicted him of operating an unlicensed money-transmitting business, but deadlocked on their far more serious money laundering and sanctions charges. His retrial on those counts has been pushed back to April of 2027. And then we have the founders of Samurai Wallet, a Bitcoin privacy wallet, who were arrested in April of 2024. Keyon Rodriguez and William Hill both pleaded guilty to the unlicensed money-transmitting charge and were sentenced to 5 years and 4 years respectively. So, the question hanging over the whole industry was unmistakable. Is writing privacy code a crime? Now, on the code itself, the government actually lost. In 2024, the Fifth Circuit ruled that the Treasury had overstepped its authority, because immutable smart contracts can't be owned. So, they can't be sanctioned as property.

[06:24] Okay, so, if the state spent years going after privacy, why are privacy-related coins all over your X.com feed again? Well, the first reason is that the tech evolved. Zero-knowledge proofs, or ZK proofs, let you prove something is true without revealing the information behind it.
[06:42] For a long time, they mostly stayed isolated in academic papers. Then, Ethereum needed to scale, and ZK rollups like ZK Sync and StarkNet put that theory into practice to verify thousands of transactions at once. And the tools built to make Ethereum faster turned out to be just as good at making it private. Which brings us to Ethereum's leadership, who have moved privacy right up the roadmap. Vitalik Buterin now sees privacy as a core pillar of the network alongside censorship resistance and security, not an optional extra. Last year, Ethereum's long-running research team was rebranded as the privacy stewards of Ethereum. And the Ethereum Foundation formed a dedicated privacy cluster of cryptographers and engineers. Then, at DevCon 2025, Buterin demoed Kohaku, an open-source framework for building privacy into everyday wallets. The Ethereum Foundation has even chipped in for the legal defenses of both Percev and Storm. And the market has been telling the same story through Zcash, which has been
[07:46] displaying an absolutely insane bull trend over the last year or so. But the biggest shift has nothing to do with cypherpunks at all. The new people demanding privacy are actually the suits. No bank is going to tokenize its trades on a ledger where every competitor can watch its positions. And no company is going to run payroll on a chain that shows the entire office what everyone else earns. That's an HR crisis waiting to happen. So, the big money has been building its own way around transparency. JP Morgan's blockchain unit, Conexus, builds confidential infrastructure because banks won't expose their strategies on public chains. In January, it announced plans to issue JP Emcoin on Canton Network, a blockchain built with configurable privacy for institutional finance. And Canton counts Goldman Sachs, BNP Paribus, BNY Mellon, and DTCC among the institutions using it. So, in the space of just a few years, privacy went from something only criminals want to a requirement for Wall Street. Funny how that works. But that raises an obvious
[08:53] question: privacy for whom? Because on one side, you have the transparent camp. That's regulators, compliance teams, and the analytics firms whose entire business is reading the ledger. And it includes stablecoin issuers, who can freeze funds with a few lines of code. Tether freezes addresses proactively and has locked up billions of dollars over the years, while Circle freezes USDC when a court order or sanction requires it. The Genius Act wrote that power into US law, obliging stablecoin issuers to be able to freeze tokens under lawful authority. Nobody in that camp is in any hurry to give that power up. On the other side, though, you have the privacy camp. That's protocol builders, developers, and ordinary users who don't want to broadcast their finances to the entire internet.
[09:40] Now, what's being built between them is something called selective disclosure. In other words, you reveal exactly what someone needs to see and nothing else. Privacy pools, which launched on Ethereum mainnet in 2025, let users prove their funds don't come from hacks or sanctioned addresses without exposing their entire history. Zcash's viewing keys let you open your transactions to an auditor or a tax authority while everyone else sees nothing. And ZK compliance proofs let you show that you've passed identity checks without handing over who you are. The SEC's crypto task force actually held a roundtable on exactly this concept just last year. But as great as this development is, selective disclosure doesn't end the fight over privacy. Because the question then becomes who holds the key, so to speak. Institutions want privacy from their competitors, but they want to stay compliant with regulators, at least for the most part.
