# One Signature. $10 Billion Gone. DeFi’s Worst Nightmare

[00:00] On the 18th of April, 2026, at approximately 1735 UTC, an attacker called a single function on a Layer Zero contract and walked away with 116,500 RS ETH worth approximately $293 million from KelpDAO, making this the largest DeFi exploit of the year so far. Within minutes, that stolen collateral had already been weaponized across Aave, Compound and Euler to borrow another quarter of a billion dollars in clean ETH. And within hours, panicked users had stripped over $6 billion of liquidity from Aave alone, withdrawals had been frozen across the entire ecosystem, and more than $10 billion from DeFi's overall TVL had been erased. Yep, more money has been vaporized, more bad headlines have been generated. And guess what? A foundational flaw in DeFi's architecture has been laid bare for good measure. My name is Guy, and you're watching The Coin Bureau.
[01:03] Let's kick off the post-mortem by looking at the asset class at the center of this disaster.
[01:09] KelpDAO is a liquid restaking protocol built on top of Eigenlayer, and its flagship token, RSETH, represents ETH that has been deposited, staked, and then restaked across additional security services to generate layered yield. Now, liquid restaking tokens, or LRTs, exploded across 2024 and 2025 as the most aggressive yield primitive in DeFi, attracting billions in deposits on the assumption that the underlying ETH collateral could always be recovered by the user. The 116,500 RSETH drained from Kelp's bridge represented roughly 18% of the entire RSETH circulating supply, and crucially, every single token was meant to be backed one for one by ETH sitting in the protocol's reserves. To move RSETH between the Ethereum mainnet and other chains like Unichain, Kelp deployed an Omnichain fungible token or OFT adapter built on Layer 0, the dominant cross-chain messaging protocol in the space. Now, the integrity of that bridge depends entirely on a component called the Decentralized
[02:23] Verifier Network or DVN, a set of independent validators tasked with confirming that whatever happens on the source chain did actually happen before the destination chain releases any tokens. And this is where the entire incident actually begins. You see, Layer 0 allows protocols to configure how many DVN signatures are required to validate a cross-chain message, and the framework permits configurations all the way down to a single signature from one single validator. Kelp DAO, despite securing close to a 3rd of a billion dollars worth of bridged collateral, had configured its bridge with a 1-of-1 DVN setup.
[03:08] So, put simply, the protocol delegated the entire security of the bridge to one signing key controlled by one entity.
[03:18] Slow Mist, the security firm that performed the post-mortem, described this configuration as the weakest security level the Layer 0 framework permits.
[03:27] And the red flag had been there in plain sight for over a year. References in Aave governance forums, dating back to January 2025, reportedly flagged this exact single validator risk when RS ETH was first being evaluated as collateral. The warning was acknowledged, debated, and then ultimately ignored by the very protocols that would later feel the consequences. Now, the attack itself unfolded with surgical precision. Roughly 10 hours before the exploit, the perpetrator funded nine separate operational wallets through Tornado Cash using the standard 1 ETH mixing pool, depositing approximately 0.0978 ETH into each address as gas. At the moment of execution, the attacker first called commit verification on the DVN verifier contract using the compromise signing key, planting a forged attestation that claimed a legitimate deposit had occurred on Unichain. Seconds later, they invoked the LZ receive function on Layer 0's Endpoint V2 contract at Ethereum block 24,908,285, delivering a payload that spoofed a deposit instruction from Kelp's peer contract on the source chain.
[04:46] The mainnet OFT adapter, seeing what appeared to be a fully attested cross-chain message, executed exactly as designed and released the entire stolen position from escrow to the attacker's address. Now, no ETH was ever locked or burned anywhere on Unichain. The tokens were, for all practical purposes, conjured directly out of thin air. As the developer 0xNGMI summarized on X, one signature and the entire stolen tranche of RSETH materialized on Ethereum, and the smart contracts themselves were never broken because the verification logic was the flaw. Now, you might assume that an attacker sitting on this scale of freshly minted unbacked tokens would immediately race to a decentralized exchange, dump the supply, and convert their bounty before anyone could react. However, that assumption entirely misunderstands the level of sophistication on display here.
[05:43] Dumping the position on the open market would have crashed the price within minutes, signalling the exploit to every monitoring tool in the industry and generating catastrophic slippage that would have eaten a significant chunk off the take. No, instead, the attacker did something far more devastating.
[06:01] They deposited the entire stolen position as collateral into RVE V3, RVE V4, Compound V3, and Euler simultaneously.
[06:11] And against that collateral, they borrowed over $236 million in wrapped ETH, or WETH, directly from the lending pools. So, clean, liquid, fully fungible ETH walked out the front door, while the RSETH positions were left behind as uncollectible bad debt for the protocols to absorb.
[06:33] And this pattern of laundering stolen illiquid collateral through lending markets is rapidly becoming the signature playbook of sophisticated DeFi exploits, and it converts a theft of a fragile asset into clean capital that can be moved anywhere on-chain. Now, KelpDAO's emergency multisig executed the "pause all" function at 1821 UTC, exactly 46 minutes after the first malicious transaction settled, freezing the deposit pool, the withdrawal module, the oracle, and the RSETH token across mainnet, arbitrum, base, and scroll.
[07:10] And that decision proved decisive. At 1826, and again at 1828, the attacker attempted two follow-up drains of 40,000 RSETH each, additional positions worth roughly $100 million apiece.
[07:25] Both transactions reverted against the active pause, blocking what would have been an additional couple of hundred million in losses. ZachXBT publicly flagged the Tornado Cash funding pattern shortly after, while the security firm Saivers traced the contagion path and warned that this was no longer just a protocol exploit, but had immediately metastasized into a cross-protocol contagion event.
[07:50] The challenge, as Saivers put it, is no longer simply preventing exploits at the contract level, but understanding how violently they can cascade across integrated protocols. And the cascade is the real story here. Aave's governance had previously approved RSETH as collateral with a loan-to-value ratio reported at approximately 93%, a buffer of seven percentage points between the borrowed amount and a position becoming under-collateralized. Under normal market conditions, that thin margin is defensible because liquidators can step in and seize collateral before bad debt accumulates. But when the underlying backing of the asset evaporates entirely because the bridge that minted it has been compromised, no liquidator on earth has any economic incentive to seize tokens worth a fraction of the borrowed sum.
[08:44] The buffer collapses instantly, and the protocol is left with the deficit. Mark Zeller of the Aave-Chan initiative, one of the most prominent governance voices in the entire ecosystem, immediately took to X urging users to withdraw their WETH from Aavev3 core now and ask questions later. The solidity auditor 0xQuit posted moments later that WETH on Aavev was, in his words, "finished" and that anyone still inside should withdraw if they could, but it was likely already too late.
[09:15] And what followed was a textbook bank run. Between 5.4 and 6.6 billion dollars in ETH was pulled from Aavev in a matter of hours, pushing pool utilization to 100% and temporarily freezing legitimate withdrawals for users who had nothing whatsoever to do with RSETH.
[09:25] Aavev's TVL collapsed from 26.4 billion dollars to roughly 20 billion dollars vaporizing approximately 6 billion dollars in a single trading session.
[09:45] Bad debt estimates across Aavev alone landed somewhere between 177 and 200 million dollars, with the figure climbing past a quarter of a billion once compound and Euler exposures were included.
[09:58] The Aavev token itself opened the day at almost 115 dollars and closed the following session at just over 91 dollars, a drop of over 20% across two trading days against an ETH market that fell less than 3% in the same window.
[10:15] SparkLend, Fluid and Upshift all froze their RSETH markets, Lido pours deposits into its earned ETH product, and Athena halted its own Layer 0 OFT bridges as a purely precautionary measure even though it had no direct exposure.
[10:33] Total DeFi TVL bled out by more than 10 billion dollars in roughly 24 hours.
[10:39] And amidst all of this, Justin Sun produced one of the most surreal performances of the entire incident.
[10:45] The Tron founder withdrew 65,584 ETH from Aavev during the height of the panic, an amount valued at approximately 154 million dollars, materially contributing to the very utilization spike that locked other users out of the protocol.
[11:03] Then, while that withdrawal was still settling, he published a public message addressed directly to the attacker on X, asking how much they wanted and suggesting it simply was not worth sacrificing both Aave and KelpDAO.
[11:18] Yes, one of the largest single drains on the liquidity pool was simultaneously positioning himself as the one to mediate the crisis.
[11:26] Only in crypto folks, only in crypto.
[11:30] And this brings us to what the 18th of April actually exposed.
[11:35] The mechanism that was exploited here was not a smart contract bug, not a front-end vulnerability, not a flash loan attack and not a governance hijack.
[11:44] The mechanism was an architectural choice to delegate the security of close to a third of a billion dollars in collateral to a single signing key, layered beneath an entire stack of lending protocols that had agreed to accept the resulting tokens with virtually no safety buffer.
[12:02] Every single liquid restaking token currently sitting in a lending market across DeFi inherits the security of the bridge that minted it.
[12:12] Every single bridge built on the OFT standard with a minimal DVN configuration represents a rehearsal of this exact exploit waiting for a sufficiently motivated attacker to materialize.
[12:25] Every single lending protocol that has approved an LRT as collateral with a thin safety margin has implicitly assumed that the underlying redemption guarantee will hold under all market conditions, including the condition where the redemption infrastructure itself has been compromised.
[12:43] The composability that makes DeFi powerful, the ability for one protocol's output to seamlessly become another protocol's input, is the exact same property that turns a single bridge failure into a multi-billion dollar ecosystem-wide rupture in under four hours.
