Now for context, Bitcoin is widely regarded as the most decentralized network in the world.
[01:15] That's because no central authority controls the network or your funds. Anyone around the world can participate and anyone can operate a node or even become a Bitcoin miner to help secure the network.
[01:28] However, Bitcoin mining ain't what it used to be. Back in the day, anyone could help process BTC transactions on a regular computer and earn BTC as a reward. But as these rewards became more lucrative, competition quickly ramped up. And today, dedicated machines called application-specific integrated circuits, or ASICs, dominate mining. Each unit costs thousands of dollars up front and even more in energy costs. And this is where Bitcoin's decentralization cracks begin to show. Put simply, the little guy has been priced out, while massive mining firms have invested billions to build operations operations with thousands of ASICs. As a result, Bitcoin's mining power is now concentrated in just a few companies, arguably undermining its decentralization. To really put things into perspective, take a second to consider that about a third of Bitcoin's mining pools are controlled by Foundry USA. Meanwhile, F2 pool controls almost 14% and Ant pool over 12%.
[02:36] So, a few large mining pools now account for a majority of Bitcoin block production, and that certainly impacts the image of Bitcoin's decentralization. To put that into perspective, hackers stole a combined $169 million across all DeFi protocols in Q1, spread over 34 separate incidents. Drift alone blew past that total in a single attack. Now, unsurprisingly, the incident sparked plenty of controversy with security researchers dissecting Drift's architecture and questioning whether stronger safeguards could have

In late 2025, Drift was approached by a group from what seemed to be a legitimate quant trading firm. Multiple meetings took place, telegram groups were created, and messages were exchanged. And this isn't just theoretical.
